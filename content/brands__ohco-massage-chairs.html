Key terms

Ultra-premium tier
The highest price band of massage chairs, where design, materials and finish are emphasized alongside the massage itself. A higher price doesn’t automatically mean a better fit for your body.
White-glove delivery
A delivery service where the chair is brought inside, unpacked, assembled and positioned for you, with packaging removed. On a heavy, design-led chair this is usually worth confirming in writing.
Body scan
A pre-program step where the chair maps your back to position the rollers. Worth testing on each model, since scan accuracy varies by frame and by your own height and build.
Roller path
The track the massage mechanism follows along your spine and legs. How it feels on your specific body matters more than the marketing name given to it.
Total ownership cost
The full cost over time: the chair, delivery, installation, any extended coverage, and likely service or repair down the line — not just the sticker price.

What OHCO massage chairs are generally known for

OHCO is typically positioned as an ultra-premium, design-forward brand — the kind of chair people search for when they want a massage chair that also reads as a piece of furniture. Buyers often look at this brand when materials, finish and a restrained aesthetic matter to them, and when they’re comparing the top of the market rather than value tiers. Searches commonly cluster around the OHCO M8, the newer M8 NEO and the M8 NEO LE, the R6, and the M.DX, often alongside questions about price and whether the premium is justified.

That luxury framing is genuine, but it’s also where the main buying risk lives. Polish can outrun fit: a beautifully finished chair that doesn’t suit your height, shoulder width or pressure preference is still the wrong chair. The most useful way to read OHCO is to acknowledge the design quality, then set it aside and ask the same practical questions you’d ask of any chair — how it feels after ten minutes, how it fits your room, and how delivery, installation, warranty and service actually work. Understanding OHCO models

The names you’ll see searched most are the OHCO M8, the M8 NEO and M8 NEO LE, the R6, and the M.DX. We list them here as recognition aids, not as reviews — we have not tested every model, and we don’t rate them. Warranty, service and delivery questions to ask

On an ultra-premium chair, the answers to these questions can matter as much as the massage. Get them in writing before you commit — a heavy, design-led chair is expensive to move and to repair, so clarity now prevents costly surprises later.

Questions to ask about warranty, service and delivery before buying an OHCO chair.
Question to askWhy it matters
Who handles service?Confirm whether the brand, the seller or a third party handles repairs, and how to reach them.
What does the warranty cover?Check parts, labor and structure separately — coverage and length often differ by component.
Is labor included?A parts-only warranty can still leave you paying for a technician’s time.
Is in-home service available?For a heavy chair, find out whether a technician comes to you or you must ship it.
What if it arrives damaged?Know the process and timeline for a damaged-on-arrival chair before you accept delivery.

Will it fit your doorways and stairs?Measure entry points; a large chair can stall at a narrow door or tight stairwell.
What are the return and cancellation terms?Know the window, any restocking fee and who pays return freight before you buy.

Reading OHCO’s wellness and feature claims

Premium chairs are sometimes described with language about advanced massage technology, intelligent body scanning or recovery. Treat those terms as prompts to verify rather than proof of benefit. If a feature is named — a particular roller system, a scan, a “recovery” program — ask what it actually does for your comfort in the chair, and judge it by how it feels, not by the marketing vocabulary. Calm, modest wording about comfort and relaxation is a good sign; dramatic health promises are a reason to slow down.

Note

Be cautious with any chair described as “medical-grade,” “clinically proven,” “doctor recommended,” “FDA approved,” or claimed to “improve circulation,” “relieve sciatica” or treat any condition — verify exactly what such phrases mean and who is making them. Reviews and showroom testing can help buyers evaluate comfort, but they should not be treated as medical evidence. Buyers with diagnosed conditions should consult a healthcare professional.
